A Quick Reference — Component Breakdown
Coordinating
Join equal elements — mnemonic: FANBOYS TYPE-01
forandnor butoryetso

Connect two independent clauses, words, or phrases of equal grammatical rank. Use a comma before the conjunction when joining two full independent clauses.

Contrast

She trained hard, but she lost.

Addition

He sings and plays guitar.

Result

It rained, so we stayed in.

Neg. Alternative

She neither laughed nor smiled.

Subordinating
Join a dependent clause to a main clause TYPE-02
althoughbecause whenif unlessuntil even thoughwhereas so that
sincewhile wheneverafter beforeonce as soon asprovided that as ifas long as

If the dependent clause comes first, separate it with a comma. If it comes second, no comma is needed.

Cause

Because she trained daily, she won.

Condition

We'll cancel unless it stops.

Concession

Although it was late, he worked on.

Contrast

She's outgoing, whereas he's shy.

Correlative
Paired conjunctions — always used together TYPE-03
both … and either … or neither … nor not only … but also whether … or as … as

Both elements must appear. The items they connect must be grammatically parallel — noun + noun, verb phrase + verb phrase, clause + clause.

Both … and

Both the teacher and the students were surprised.

Not only … but also

She not only wrote it but also performed it.

Either … or

You can either call or send an email.

Neither … nor

Neither the director nor the actors knew.

!

The Comma Rule — Specification Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction when it joins two complete independent clauses (She was tired, but she continued). No comma when it joins only words or phrases (bread and cheese). With subordinating conjunctions: comma when the dependent clause comes first (Because it rained, we stayed in) — no comma when it comes second (We stayed in because it rained).

B Combine & Complete
1
TYPE-02 · Cause
The match was postponed. There was a storm forecast.

Combine using a subordinating conjunction. Give both word orders.

Type first, then check
Because there was a storm forecast, the match was postponed.
The match was postponed because there was a storm forecast.

Clause-first → comma required. Clause-second → no comma.

2
TYPE-01 · Contrast
The documentary was long. It was absolutely gripping.

Combine into one sentence using an appropriate coordinating conjunction.

Type first, then check
The documentary was long, but it was absolutely gripping.

Two independent clauses joined by but → comma before the conjunction.

3
TYPE-03 · not only … but also
She speaks French. She also writes poetry in French.

Rewrite using not only … but also. Keep parallel structure.

Type first, then check
She not only speaks French but also writes poetry in it.

Both slots take verb phrases → parallel. "In it" avoids repeating "in French."

4
TYPE-02 · Concession
He had very little time to prepare. His speech was brilliant.

Combine using a subordinating conjunction expressing concession.

Type first, then check
Although he had very little time to prepare, his speech was brilliant.
or: Even though he had very little time to prepare, his speech was brilliant.

Although / even though / though all work. Despite / in spite of are prepositions — they require a noun phrase or gerund, not a full clause.

5
TYPE-03 · neither … nor
The heating isn't working. The hot water isn't working either.

Rewrite using neither … nor.

Type first, then check
Neither the heating nor the hot water is working.

Neither … nor is already negative — do not add not elsewhere. Verb agrees with the nearer subject (hot water → singular → is).

6
TYPE-02 · Condition
You must submit the form. Otherwise you will lose your place.

Combine using unless.

Type first, then check
Unless you submit the form, you will lose your place.
or: You will lose your place unless you submit the form.

Unless = if … not. Never write unless you don't — that reverses the meaning.

7
TYPE-02 · Time
We will wait. The results are announced.

Join using an appropriate time conjunction.

Type first, then check
We will wait until the results are announced.

After future time conjunctions (until, when, as soon as, before, after), use simple present — not future — in the subordinate clause.

C Common Mistakes — Error Log
1
✕ Although she was tired, but she kept going.
✓ Although she was tired, she kept going.
Although already introduces concession. Adding but creates a double conjunction — choose one or the other, never both.
2
✕ Despite it was raining, we went out.
✓ Despite the rain, we went out. / Although it was raining, we went out.
Despite / in spite of are prepositions — noun or gerund only, not a full clause. Use although / even though for a full clause.
3
✕ Unless you don't hurry, you'll miss the bus.
✓ Unless you hurry, you'll miss the bus.
Unless already means if not. Adding don't creates a double negative that reverses the meaning.
4
✕ Neither the manager or the staff were informed.
✓ Neither the manager nor the staff were informed.
Neither pairs only with nor; either pairs only with or. Cross-pairing is a very common error.
5
✕ We will call you when we will arrive.
✓ We will call you when we arrive.
In time clauses with when, until, after, before, as soon as, use simple present — not future — even when the meaning is future.
D Spot & Correct — One Error Per Line
1
She not only sings beautifully but she also dances professionally.
Type first, then check
She not only sings beautifully but also dances professionally.

Not only … but also — inserting she between but and also breaks parallel structure.

2
He works hard, so but he never seems to get ahead.
Type first, then check
He works hard, yet he never seems to get ahead.

Two coordinating conjunctions cannot be used together. Yet (concessive contrast) is the correct single conjunction here.

3
Because of she missed the bus, she arrived late.
Type first, then check
Because she missed the bus, she arrived late.

Because is a conjunction — it introduces a full clause directly. Because of is a preposition requiring a noun phrase: Because of the delay…

4
Both the food and the service was excellent.
Type first, then check
Both the food and the service were excellent.

Both … and always takes a plural verb — both subjects are included.

5
Whereas I enjoy spicy food but my sister finds it unbearable.
Type first, then check
Whereas I enjoy spicy food, my sister finds it unbearable.

Whereas already sets up the contrast. The main clause does not need but as well.

6
I'll lend you the money provided that you will pay me back by Friday.
Type first, then check
I'll lend you the money provided that you pay me back by Friday.

Provided that introduces a conditional clause — takes simple present, not future.

1 Which sentence uses a coordinating conjunction correctly? Easy
AAlthough she trained hard but she lost.
BShe trained hard, yet she lost.
CShe trained hard yet but lost.
DShe trained hard, although lost.
Yet joins two independent clauses in contrast. A comma precedes it because both clauses are complete. Option A uses two conjunctions (although … but) — pick one only.
2 Choose the correct sentence: Easy
ADespite the traffic was bad, we arrived on time.
BDespite of the heavy traffic, we arrived on time.
CDespite the heavy traffic, we arrived on time.
DIn spite that traffic was bad, we arrived on time.
Despite is a preposition → noun phrase only. In spite of requires of. Although / even though take a full clause.
3 Which sentence uses a time conjunction correctly? Medium
ACall me when you will arrive.
BI'll start eating until you come back.
CAs soon as the results will be published, I'll let you know.
DAs soon as the results are published, I'll let you know.
After future time conjunctions, use simple present in the subordinate clause — not future. Option B misuses until: it means up to a point, not after it.
4 "I'll go to the party __ I finish this report." — Which conjunction fits best? Medium
Abecause
Bonce
Calthough
Dwhereas
Once signals that the first action must be completed before the second can happen. The other options express cause, concession, and contrast respectively.
5 Which sentence uses not only … but also with correct parallel structure? Medium
AHe not only fixed the engine but also the brakes were repaired by him.
BHe not only fixed the engine but also to repair the brakes.
CHe not only fixed the engine but also repaired the brakes.
DHe not only fixed the engine but also he repaired the brakes.
C is correct: not only + verb phrase (fixed the engine), but also + verb phrase (repaired the brakes) — both slots hold the same grammatical structure. A switches to passive voice, breaking parallelism. B uses an infinitive where a finite verb is required. D inserts a redundant subject he, disrupting the paired structure.
6 "Neither the CEO __ the board members __ present at the vote." — Correct form? Hard
Aor … was
Bnor … were
Cor … were
Dnor … was
Neither … nor is the fixed pair. Verb agrees with the nearer subject (board members → plural → were).
7 Which sentence uses so that correctly? Hard
AShe left early, so that she was tired.
BHe studies so that he studied well.
CThey whispered so that interrupted the meeting.
DShe left early so that she could catch the last train.
So that expresses purpose — it must be followed by a clause with could / would / can / will / might. Do not confuse with so (result).
